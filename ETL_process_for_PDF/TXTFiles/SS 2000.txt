Barkmeijer 3200 class of sea/river multi-pur- pose cargo vessel represents one of the most suc- cessful designs in its category, with 18 units expected to be in service by the end of 2000 - seven of which are owned and/or operated by Arklow Shipping. The low- profile, single-deck design features a raised forecastle and sunken poop, with a double-skinned hull which provides side and double-bottom water ballast tanks.
Within this layout the single 63m x 10.20m wide hold is box-shaped and obstruction-ftee, and closed by a hatch cover made up of 10 steel pontoons, handled by a
Roden Staal electro-hydraulic gantry crane travelling along the hatch coamings.

This crane is also used to move two bulkheads which can be positioned in the hold to serve as cargo divisions.
Of double-skin steel construction, these are secured by slotting into container corner fittings in the tank top, thus allowing them to be erected in steps of 20ft throughout the hold. When not in use, the divisions are stowed where they do not encroach on the cargo space.
The tank top has a permissable loading of 12tonnes/m?* and is strengthened for the carriage of steel coils each weighing 25tonnes. Container capacity in the hold is
T6TEU, with 40TEU carried on the hatch covers.

The hull-form has been optimised around maximum cargo capacity and features an underwater shape, aft of the forward engineroom bulkhead, which incorporates an integrated tunnel configuration which encloses the single FP propeller, fitted into a clearwater sternframe.

The tunnel arrangement is designed to enhance propul- sive efficiency by improving water flow from the sides of the aft body to the propeller, and is understood to promote better propeller immersion in different load states in rough water.

The machinery installation is centred upon a Mak 8M20 main engine developing 1360kW at 1000rev/min; this is resiliently mounted to reduce noise levels. The drive to the five-bladed propeller is by way of a Reintjes WAF 1142 reversing reduction gear- box, and Vulkan Rato 2120 flexible couplings.
Electrical power comes from two Valmet/Stamford 112kVA diesel-alternator sets. It is noteworthy that the last (seventh) vessel in the Arklow-class has a modified propulsion arrangement with a CP propeller and shaft alternator included. Dutch manufacturer Barkemeyer supplied the steering gear and the flap-type rudder, with manoeuvrabilty assisted by a 200kW Veth bow thruster, powered by its own Scania diesel engine.

Accommodation is arranged in the poop and poop deckhouse for a complement of seven, all occupying single rooms - although captain, mate, engineer and spare cabins do have twin beds. Plywood/ multiplex and
Vermipan panels have been used for cabin bulkheads in accordance with SOLAS Method III-C fire regulations, and these also provide optimum acoustic insulation.

IHE economic importance of Russia's northern
 territories, and the ice breaker-assisted polar sea route which sustains them, have long been recog- nised. Now, the prospect of an export trade generated by the vast oil and gas reserves in the region has given fresh impetus to a number of projects aimed at expanding navigation along the northern Russia coast through the North-East Passage, which has led to the introduction of new ship designs able to combat the severe conditions experienced at these latitudes.

Whilst the more sophisticated of these designs, notably Kvaerner Masa-Yards' DAT (double-acting tanker) with Azipod propulsion, will not be in service for at least two years, others of a more conventional
Arctic concept have recently been delivered and, like
Astrakhan, these include the latest Russian technology in this field. This vessel is of usual double-hull tanker layout with single deck, poop and forecastle, but, her structure has been strengthened for navigation in ice to
Russian Marine Register Class YA , and she is capable of travelling in even, 0.5m thick (‘solid’) ice, at a speed of 2knots. ‘

The icebreaker stem is inclined at 40deg and the tran- som stern is fitted with an ice knife. Machinery and accommodation are arranged right aft, with the latter designed to provide comfortable conditions for a crew of 23.

A centreline bulkhead and seven transverse bulk- heads, all of troughed construction, divide the cargo space into 16 tanks arranged to carry oil products of four grades simultaneously, having densities between

LTHOUGH the annual rate of expansion of the

British cruise market is currently declining, the number of passengers enjoying this type of vacation, now standing at over 800,000 annually, is some four times greater than it was just a decade ago. This fact justifies P&O's decision to build what is basically a modified sister ship to the now five-year-old Oriana (Significant Ships of 1995), and sailing her out of
Southampton between May and December each yeat.
She will run a series of Mediterranean and European cruises, followed by an annual three-month world voy- age, serving a predominantly British clientele.

Slightly longer than Oriana, Aurora exhibits what
P&O describes as a 'more relaxed and contemporary feel', both internally and externally, than her sister, which reflects the rapid changes in passenger expecta- tions - especially from the 'over fifties! - which have occurred even in the time since Oriana was completed.

The major difference between the two ships lies in the choice of main machinery: this time P&O has opted for a full diesel-electric system, rather than the complex, but versatile 'father-and-son' geared diesel

 arrangement plus 'electric assist' of the earlier vessel.
Four ABB synchronous generator sets, each driven by a MAN B&W 14V48/60 diesel engine and delivering 14,700kW at 514rev/min, provide primary electrical power, serving both ship's services and two 20,000kW
STN/AEG propulsion motors; the lattter are directly coupled to a pair of FP propellers. Two bow thrusters, one stern thruster, and two spade rudders, are all con- trolled by joystick.

A Siemens automation system consists of both sys- tem and field bus levels, with I/O units and some inde- pendent systems such as propulsion, main switchboard, fresh water evaporators, and watertight door operation, connected to the self-sufficient main process stations.
These are controlled and monitored from the engine control room, the bridge, and some offices. Integrated into the overall automation system are general moni- toring and alarms; emergency shut-down; safety sys- tems for the diesel engines; complete power manage- ment; temperature and pressure controls; tank level and draught gauge systems; and air conditioning.
Navigation is centred on an Atlas NACOS 65-3 inte- grated bridge system including Multi Pilot IT.

Total passenger complement is 2290, reduced with double occupancy to 1878, berthed in 623 outside and 286 inside cabins. In addition, there are 30 suites of one form or another, including two penthouse units built over two decks with a unique upstairs bedroom, all of which, plus 376 outside cabins, have balconies.
Disabled passengers have 22 special cabins, eight of which are provided with balconies, and there are also four single cabins. Public spaces occupy decks 5 to 8, which is the extent of an atrium having natural stone walls and columns, a waterfall statue and a main stait- case with bronze handrails. Deck 12 contains the fit- ness centre and Orangery self-service restaurant. Deck 13 is the location of the Crow's Nest observation
 lounge and disco. A total of 10 MacGregor passenger lifts serve the whole arrangement.

The two main restaurants on deck 6 seat approxi- mately 1070 passengers, whilst Carmen's Lounge on deck 7 has a decorative dance floor and 400 seats. At the forward end of this deck, the fully equipped
Curzon Theatre can entertain 700, whilst Anderson's ‘typical British pub' is also on this deck. Shops, bars, a casino, features for children aged 2 to 17, and three outdoor pools - one having a sliding roof - are amongst other facilities available in a design scheme originated by leading international practitioners Tillberg, SMC, Yran & Storbraaten, and McNeece, and displaying
British-style decoration.

HIS unusual combination tanker, which extends

ENVC's long experience of advanced tanker con- struction to new levels, is intended for operations around the American coasts in support of her owner's manufacturing interests. A double-hulled vessel with combined side, bottom and topside wing tanks, Aurora conforms to conventional tanker external appearance, but has a cargo space divided into six holds by horizon- tally corrugated transverse bulkheads. The forward, midships, and aft compartments thus formed are each fitted with a mild-steel, free-standing tank for the trans- port of liquid sulphur, whilst the remaining three spaces form centrally divided, stainless-steel, integral tanks arranged for the carriage of acids, normally of the sul- phuric and phosphoric types.

The sulphur tanks are mounted to allow expansion and contraction according to temperature conditions, the cargo being carried at 150°C, a temperature which is maintained by means of thermal insulation. This is composed of Rockwool slabs sheathed with galvanised steel plates of approximately 0.5mm thickness, and designed to ensure that the temperature loss is no more than 10°C in 10 days.

The cylindrical 'dome', which forms part of each sul- phur tank, is similarly insulated, with the upper deck penetration made watertight by means of a synthetic rubber skirt. Cargo and thermal oil piping is of the pre- insulated type, using high- density Rockwool combined with a Rockwool/polyurethane mix, and the entire sul- phur cargo system is heated by a thermal-oil installa- tion, which includes provision of jackets for pumps and valves.

Both the thermal-oil lines and those for cargo are of LR Industti's pre-insulated type. The polyurethane foam insulation includes a tracer pipe with a self- regulating heating cable for optimum heat transfer.
The heating system uses straight, plain black heating coils with a capacity to compensate for estimated cargo heat losses; each tank has its own independent system.

Six sets of Deswall deepwell pumps (one for each tank), with a capacity of 100m*/h handle acid cargoes, with six more (two for each tank) fitted in the sulphur tanks and rated at 150m*/h. Two 80m°/h centrifugal pumps serve cylindrical slop tanks mounted on deck.
Separate manifolds are provided for each cargo, and venting systems are fitted to each tank, together with a
 14
 nitrogen padding inert-gas system supplied from two compressors. The gas inlet to the sulphur tanks enters through the dome and is inclined to the centre of the tank to avoid formation of gas pockets due to the ship's trim. Cargo and ballast monitoring, control and opera- tion, together with radar-type sounding and remote valve operation, all with appropriate alarms, are carried out from the cargo control room. The acid tanks are fit- ted with a cold fresh water washing system using a 150m‘/h pump, discharging into the slop tanks, thence through the manifolds to shore.

The machinery installation is centred on a Kawasaki-
MAN B&W 6S46MC-C main engine developing 7860kW at 126rev/min, and electrical power is derived from three Ssanyong-MAN B&W/Hyundai 659kW alternator sets.

HE re-entry of Gdynia Shipyard into the LPG mat-
 ket after an absence of more than 20 years, and the return of the Bergesen Group to take its first new- building from the yard for 17 years, are significant markers to attach to the delivery of Berge Danuta and her sister. These vessels which, along with two other entries in this Year 2000 review, demonstrate the variety of out- put from the Gdynia shipyard, which is today able to use the recently purchased, former Stocznia Gdanska premises to handle some of the 50-plus contracts cur- rently held by the parent company. It is understood that almost half of that substantial order book will be sub- contracted to the new site.

The single-skin hull of Berge Danuta has top and bot- tom water ballast wing tanks, the latter being joined with double-bottom tanks divided by a duct keel, to form common tanks, whilst side ducts form a connection between the upper and lower compartments. Transverse bulkheads divide the cargo space into four holds, each fitted with an independent, centrally divided prismatic cargo tank designed for -50°C operating temperature at approximately atmospheric pressure. These, and that part of the hull serving as a secondary barrier, have been constructed of low-temperature steel of NV 2-4 grade, supplied from the Huta Czestochowa mill, ready pre- pared to suit the Gdynia yard's welding procedures. The hullform was perfected at the CTO tank.

All structural calculations for the construction of the tanks were carried out by the shipyard using the
SESAM programme developed by Det Norske Veritas.
Insulation has been applied in accordance with the Uni- tor system, in a new purpose-built workshop, using 200mm thick polyurethane foam. A Hamworthy KSE refrigeration plant has been fitted, comprising four 222kW compressor units, arranged so that boil-off gases are returned to the tanks.

Discharge of cargo is by eight deepwell cargo pumps, with two booster pumps located on deck, all operating through the midships liquid and vapour manifolds. The piping systems make use of a lot of stainless steel pipe, much of which has been supplied by LR Industri, of
Denmark, in prefabricated and pre-insulated form, with joints constructed using automatic orbital welding.

The main engine is a Cegielski-Sulzer O(RTAGO8TB unit developing 17,640kW at 94rev/min. A Shinko 1000kW

 steam-turbo-driven alternator provides electrical power sufficient for the sea load, with three Cegielski- MAN
B&W/ABB 1000kW diesel-driven alternator sets also available. Steam is generated by an Aalborg oil-fired water-tube boiler and a Green's exhaust-gas economiset, and engineroom, and cargo handling oper- ations are fully automated using Norcontrol systems.
Firefighting complies with F-AMC-class requirements, and the navigation and communication systems are arranged to W1-OC standards for one-man-bridge operation.

These are supple- mented by the output from a Fincantieri turbine which draws steam from a waste-heat boiler fitted in the exhaust lines from the gas turbines, to drive a similar 9MW alternator.

Of the 59MW electrical power generated in this way, some 39MW is available for propulsion, with the remainder satisfying all other shipboard needs. Also available from the system is 28 tonnes/h steam utilised for fresh water production, laundry, galley, air condi- tioning, and other needs.

By combining aspects of vee-form naval hulls, sub- marine pontoons, semi-tunnel sterns, and_ utilising weight-saving high-tensile steel for all structure except- ing the funnel and masts (fabricated from aluminium), the now patented design is considered to achieve power requirement savings of between 20%-25% over con- ventional hulls having the same parameters, as well as offering important reductions in wave-making resis- tance, noise and vibration, and producing superior sea- keeping qualities.

Shipbuilder:.

HIS interesting vessel is understood to be the first
 to be fitted with a Siemens-Schottel SSP podded propulsion system, and the first to go to sea. The con- cept of installing propeller drive motors in azimuthing pods outside, and under the stern, of a vessel, was first introduced to Significant Ships readers in 1998 in a cruise- ship application (E/aton) using the Kvaerner-ABB Azi- pod system. However, the SSP unit does introduce alternative technology, in this case incorporated into a single, centreline-mounted pod with FP propellers at each end and a pair of fins on the body.

The SSP unit combines the latest Siemens research into permanent-magnet electric motors (PEM), utilis- ing new materials for the magnet, with Schottel's twin- propeller technology (STP), where two propellers run on the same shaft, and rotate in the same direction, in a fully azimuthing pod fitted with fins. This system is claimed to minimise space requitements and to bring benefits of progressive motor control and energy man- agement, whilst producing lower specific loadings, higher efficiency, less noise and vibration, weight reduc- tion, and energy saving over competitive designs.

A particular feature of the pod concept is, of course, that it does not require installation until late in the con- struction schedule. It can also be removed for mainte- nance or repair, and refitted whilst the vessel is afloat.

Prospero's installation uses a SSP7-T unit suspended under a barge-type underwater stern configuration, which has a central skeg faired into the lines of the propulsor for efficient water flow. A slim-line motor ensures the underwater housing does not provide any significant obstruction in the propeller area, where each 4800mm diameter propeller has three FP blades.

This system gives a guaranteed speed at 9.25m draught of 14.50knots, with the SSP unit developing
 86
 5100kW at 124rev/min. Electrical power for propulsion and all ship's services comes from four alternators, posi- tioned right-aft in the main hull; each is each fitted with a Wartsila 9L20 prime mover, and is rated at 1620kW/1000rev/min.

Within a double hull, the ship is divided by an arrangement of vertically troughed centreline, and transverse bulkheads, into 14 epoxy-coated tanks suit- able for Type II cargoes, having a total capacity of 20,200m', and handled by an outfit of submerged cen- trifugal pumps. Water ballast is carried in the double- hull space, and in dedicated tanks in accordance with normal practice.

The hull and necessary equipment is ice-strengthened to Class 1A, Northern Baltic/Finnish/Swedish stan- dards, and the full, square, transom stern allows an ample deckhouse to be built on the poop to provide accommodation for a complement of 15 persons, served by a stern-mounted free-fall lifeboat.

The hullform features a U-shaped forebody with bul- bous bow, and V-shaped aft body having a central skeg and propeller tunnels to provide a high speed for deliv- eries to dumping grounds, an ability to dredge in shal- low water with low wash, and a good inflow to the propellers. This latter space has been built without any sub-divi- sion but is considered not to provide any free surface ptoblems due to its exceptional width and the vessel's deep freeboard.

Aft of the hopper, the dredge pump room contains the two main pumps, driven via three-speed gearboxes from the forward end of two MAN B&W 14V48/60 main engines, positioned in the adjacent room.
